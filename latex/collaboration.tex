\begin{abstract}
{\noindent We examine the effects of conglomerate mergers on firm growth and the competitive landscape using an agent-based model. We provide evidence that resource sharing within conglomerates acts as a risk mitigation strategy in multiplicative growth processes, providing incentives for conglomerate formation. Our model shows that even small levels of resource sharing can significantly alter market dynamics, leading to more balanced measures of market concentration and increased firm mobility. Contrary to concerns about market concentration, our results suggest that conglomerate formation can reduce both static and dynamic measures of market concentration.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Conglomerative mergers are important to many fields of economic inquiry, ranging from industrial organization \parencite{audretsch1989determinants, goldberg1973effect, goldberg1974conglomerate}, international trade \parencite{baltagi2007estimating, ramondo2010role, lind2023trade}, to finance \parencite{lewellen1971pure, boguth2022dissecting} and even management studies \parencite{amit1988concept, amihud1981risk}. However, unlike horizontal or vertical mergers, whose drivers have been studied thoroughly, the determinants of conglomerative mergers have not received nearly as much attention. Formally, a conglomerate merger is defined as the merging of two businesses that operate in different markets and are not part of the same value chain \parencite{scherer1990industrial}. Many of these mergers take place across borders, which is why they are also known as complex foreign direct investment \parencite{baltagi2007estimating}. They also play a role in market and product extension mergers \parencite{felton1971conglomerate}. However, the determinants of such mergers remain both manifold and elusive. Among those brought forward are economies of scale and increasing market power \parencite{mueller1969theory}, taxation \parencite{cohen1969conglomerate}, or alignment with management salary risk preferences \parencite{amihud1981risk}. One overarching theme, however, is the reduction in risk. 

The literature on risk reduction as a driver of conglomerate formation is extensive. \textcite{scherer1990industrial} emphasizes a reduction in the standard deviation of profits from combining two firms operating in independent markets. \textcite{amihud1981risk} argue that managers pursue conglomerate mergers because their human capital is tied to the firm, making diversification a rational response to employment risk. \textcite{mueller1969theory} introduces growth maximization as a reason for conglomeration, where diversification across constituent firms yields an increased rate of growth for the owning entity. These contributions correctly identified risk mitigation as central to understanding conglomerate formation. However, the analytical tools they deploy---variance, standard deviation, and expected-value reasoning---are well suited to additive dynamics but miss a critical feature of firm growth: its multiplicative nature. 

Firm growth is empirically multiplicative, a regularity formalized by \textcite{gibrat1931} as the Law of Proportionate Effect and confirmed across decades of subsequent work \parencite{sutton1997gibrat, stanley1996scaling, bottazzi2006explaining, santarelli2006gibrat}. Under multiplicative dynamics, the relevant performance measure for a firm is the time-average growth rate, sometimes referred to as the geometric mean of periodic growth increments, rather than the expected value (ensemble average) \parencite{peters2019ergodicity}. Crucially, under such dynamics, variance does not merely describe the spread of outcomes around the central tendency; it directly reduces the time-average growth rate through a mechanism known as volatility drag \parencite{messmore1995variance, spitznagel2021safe}: even when gains and losses are perfectly symmetric, their compound effect is net negative because losses carry disproportionate weight under multiplication. This insight reframes the problem. The intuition of earlier work, stating that conglomeration serves as insurance against adverse outcomes, was substantively correct. What was missing was the conceptual framework appropriate for multiplicative processes, under which risk reduction translates directly into growth rate improvement, not merely into lower distributional spread (formalized in section~\ref{sec:Background}). 

Within this framework, resource sharing across constituent firms of a  conglomerate becomes a positive-sum rather than a zero-sum arrangement. The concavity of the logarithm implies that transferring resources from a better-performing to a worse-performing unit reduces the donor's growth by less than it increases the recipient's, raising the conglomerate's aggregate growth rate. \textcite{peters2022ergodicity} establish this result formally for noisy multiplicative growth, building on earlier work in physics on cooperation in random multiplicative environments \parencite{yaari2010cooperation} and on the general formulation in operations research by \textcite{liebmann2017sharing}.

This is qualitatively different from standard portfolio diversification. When shareholders hold diversified portfolios, they reduce their own exposure to firm-specific risk, but each firm's growth trajectory remains unchanged. Conglomerate formation, by contrast, enables internal resource transfers that directly alter the growth dynamics of constituent firms. We label this mechanism \textit{cooperative diversification}: a cooperative arrangement that increases the time-average growth rate of each constituent unit, rather than merely reducing variance at the portfolio level. This distinction connects naturally to the broader literature on geometric mean maximization in finance, a concept that mainstream portfolio theory accepts in principle but that has not been systematically applied to corporate structure and merger incentives. \textcite{mundt2022survival} provide independent empirical evidence that firm-level profitability exhibits the non-ergodic properties on which our mechanism depends, motivating the relevance of the framework to firms specifically. 

Our analysis engages with and extends several strands of the corporate finance literature. \textcite{lewellen1971pure} provides a static coinsurance rationale operating through one-period distributional effects that primarily benefit bondholders. Our mechanism operates through temporal compounding: under multiplicative dynamics, variance reduction directly increases the geometric growth rate of equity, independent of leverage. \textcite{stein1997internal} develops the winner-picking model of internal capital markets, where headquarters creates value by reallocating capital from weaker to stronger divisions based on information advantages. Our model complements this: even absent information asymmetries or financial frictions, sharing resources across stochastic multiplicative processes generates a growth-rate effect beyond what expected-value allocation logic captures. The ``dark side'' literature documents that divisional rent-seeking corrupts capital allocation, leading to subsidization of weaker divisions \parencite{scharfstein2000dark}, and that diversified firms misallocate capital when headquarters has limited power, producing a diversification discount \parencite{rajan2000cost}. Beyond these contributions, we identify a separate force that exists alongside agency frictions: under multiplicative dynamics, even a firm that allocates capital without any agency frictions generates an intrinsic growth synergy through pooling. Agency costs are real implementation challenges, but they do not negate the mathematical benefits of variance reduction under compounding; they are frictions that may partially offset an underlying positive-sum mechanism. \textcite{hann2013corporate} provide supportive evidence on the bright side, showing that diversified firms enjoy lower systematic risk and cost of capital through coinsurance. While conceptually aligned, their analysis is static and mean-variance based, whereas our framework captures the temporal, growth-rate dimension of risk pooling. 

The diversification-discount debate provides a useful frame for situating our contribution. Early findings suggest that diversified firms trade at a 13--15\% discount relative to focused firms \parencite{lang1994tobins, berger1995diversification}. However, this evidence has been substantially challenged. \textcite{campa2002explaining} show that the discount largely reflects endogeneity given that firms that diversify are systematically different from those that do not. \textcite{villalonga2004diversification} finds that diversified firms actually trade at a premium when measurement biases are corrected using establishment-level data. Most relevant to our framework, \textcite{kuppuswamy2016does} document that the diversification discount reversed during the 2007--2009 financial crisis, when conglomerates maintained access to internal capital while focused firms faced external financing constraints, which is precisely the setting where pooling benefits under multiplicative dynamics should manifest most strongly. We defer a fuller treatment of this debate to Section \ref{sec:Discussion}, but note that our theoretical framework provides a natural explanation for why diversification benefits are heterogeneous and state-dependent. Our analysis also relates to the multi-product firm literature, which examines how firms choose product scope \parencite{bernard2010multiple, nocke2014globalization}. However, this literature typically takes the firm boundary as given and examines internal portfolio decisions, whereas we model the formation of conglomerates through mergers of previously independent firms operating in unrelated markets. By isolating pure conglomeration through the assumption of uncorrelated markets, we identify the independent contribution of resource pooling to merger incentives, which may operate alongside other motivations in more complex merger types. We do allow for some correlation which changes the results only marginally.  

A separate question concerns the impact of conglomerate mergers on the competitive landscape. While \textcite{waldman1983impact} finds that post-acquisition firms grow at above-industry rates, \textcites{goldberg1973effect}{goldberg1974conglomerate} do not detect consequences that would increase market concentration. The results in \textcite{waldman1983impact} appear to depend on the acquiring company financially supporting the acquired company as a necessary condition for outperformance. Naturally, there is a limit to the size of a conglomerate, as management costs for coordinating all constituent businesses increase \parencite{coase19883}. This provides a natural drag on the effectiveness of conglomeration as a means to increase the growth rate. The direction of the effect on market concentration is of course relevant to policymakers, though we note that the welfare implications of concentration changes require additional assumptions about pricing and demand that our framework does not incorporate. 

We advance the answer to both, why conglomerates form and how they affect competition, through theoretical analysis and simulation. Our contributions are threefold. First, we show that the positive-sum nature of resource pooling under multiplicative dynamics, established by \textcite{peters2022ergodicity} in the context of general cooperation, constitutes a novel and self-sufficient rationale for conglomerate formation. This extends and reinterprets the risk-reduction arguments of \textcite{amihud1981risk}, \textcite{lewellen1971pure}, and \textcite{mueller1969theory} within a framework appropriate for the empirically established multiplicative nature of firm growth. Second, we distinguish between portfolio diversification and cooperative diversification through pooling, showing that the latter offers superior growth-rate optimization that cannot be replicated by shareholders diversifying their own portfolios---connecting to but going beyond the internal capital markets literature \parencite{stein1997internal, gertner1994internal}. Third, we characterize, through an agent-based model encompassing 70,200 runs, the implications of these merger incentives for conglomerate scope, capital concentration, and rank mobility, finding that the pooling mechanism in isolation tends to compress concentration and increase market mobility, contributing to the empirical debate on conglomerate effects initiated by \textcite{goldberg1973effect} and \textcite{waldman1983impact}. Importantly, the pooling mechanism we characterize is one force among several governing organizational form; selection into conglomeration, coordination costs, agency frictions, operational synergies, market correlations, and broader institutional context all enter the determination of equilibrium outcomes. Isolating the empirical signature of the pooling channel specifically would require designs that hold these competing forces fixed.

Our model sits in the agent-based tradition of industry evolution \parencite{nelson1982schumpeterian, winter2003baseline} and starts from the regularities that tradition has documented: proportional growth, tent-shaped growth-rate distributions and a Zipf tail of firm sizes \parencite{stanley1996scaling,bottazzi2006explaining,axtell2001zipf,dosi2017footprint,coad2009growth}. We extend these frameworks by allowing firms to alter the selection pressure they face, by pooling returns, through voluntary contracting, based financial analysis of their finite history.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section \ref{sec:Background} develops the concept of growth-rate optimization under multiplicative dynamics. Section \ref{sec:Model} presents the agent-based model and its assumptions. Section \ref{sec:Results} reports the simulation results, and Section \ref{sec:Discussion} puts these into context before Section \ref{sec:Conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:Background}
As outlined in the introduction, under multiplicative dynamics the standard critique that resource sharing is zero-sum no longer holds. In a setting of multiplicative dynamics, where the next state $s_{t+1}$ of some variable (cumulated capital in our case) depends on the previous one $s_t$ \parencite{gibrat1931,sutton1997gibrat}, the objective is to maximize the time-average growth rate $g$, computed as the log of the geometric average of periodic gross returns $\delta_t \in (0, \infty)$. The geometric average itself is sometimes referred to as the Compound Annual Growth Rate in investment circles.

\begin{gather}
    g = \log \Big(\prod_{t=1}^{T} \delta_t \Big)^{1/T}
\end{gather}

In investment practices, the geometric average is also known as the compound annual growth rate. In the continuous-time limit g is the drift of the log process.

The premise of multiplicative firm growth is not merely a modeling convenience but an empirically well-established regularity. The foundational work by \textcite{gibrat1931}, formalized as the Law of Proportionate Effect, posits that a firm's expected growth rate is independent of its current size. While strict Gibrat dynamics are sometimes rejected for small firms facing selection pressures, this can be interpreted within the multiplicative framework: firm exit represents a growth increment of $\delta_t \approx 0$, and small firms are more likely to draw such values due to higher variance in their growth process. \textcite{stanley1996scaling} document this variance-size relationship, showing that growth rate volatility  $\sigma$ scales with size $S$ as $\sigma \sim S^{-\beta}$ with $\beta \approx 0.15-0.20$, meaning that smaller firms experience more volatile growth and are more exposed to extreme negative realizations. This is reinforced by \textcite{lotti2009defending}, who show that initial deviations from proportionate growth converge toward Gibrat dynamics as firms mature and inefficient entrants exit, suggesting multiplicativity is the long-run regularity.

The statistical signatures of multiplicative dynamics are unambiguous. \textcite{stanley1996scaling} document that firm growth-rate distributions follow a Laplace (tent-shaped) distribution rather than a Gaussian, a pattern holding across seven orders of magnitude in firm size for U.S. manufacturing firms. \textcite{bottazzi2006explaining} confirm this regularity across Italian manufacturing sectors and provide theoretical microfoundations through competitive dynamics. These distributional properties (fat tails and exponential decay) arise naturally from multiplicative processes and cannot be generated by additive models \parencite{nair2022fundamentals}. Furthermore, \textcite{axtell2001zipf} demonstrates that U.S. firm sizes follow Zipf's Law almost exactly, a power-law distribution that emerges mathematically from multiplicative growth combined with entry and exit, as shown by \textcite{gabaix1999zipf}.

The lower the gross return, the greater its weight in the growth rate: while an increment of $\delta_t = 0.8$ already requires the next one to be $\delta_{t + 1} = 1.25$ to compensate it, one of $\delta_t = 0.5$ requires $\delta_{t+1}$ to be $2$. This asymmetry in influence of losses over gains grows exponentially and follows a logarithmic function of the increments' impact on the growth rate as seen in Figure \ref{fig:log}. 

Now suppose there are two firms $i$ and $j$ in a conglomerate and absolute increments defined as $\Delta_{it} = \delta_{it} s_{it-1} - s_{it-1}$ \parencite{sutton1997gibrat}. Furthermore, without loss of generality suppose $\Delta_{it} > \Delta_{jt}$ and a sharing amount of $x$, which is transferred from the better-performing firm $i$ to the worse-performing firm $j$. Given that $\frac{\partial^2 log(x)}{\partial^2 x} < 0$, it is clear that $\log(\Delta_{it}) - \log(\Delta_{it} - x) < \log(\Delta_{jt} + x) - \log(\Delta_{jt})$. The reduction in firm $i$'s growth is overcompensated by the increase in firm $j$'s growth, which raises the conglomerate's growth rate. This is a key result, since it is fundamentally different from conventional diversification arguments.

One can interpret this as raising the base of state $s_{jt+1}$ on which $j$'s next return acts. Extending this example to a multiplicative process consisting of normally distributed log returns of mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the time-average growth rate without sharing converges to

\begin{gather}
    \label{eq:diversification}
    g = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}
\end{gather}

In addition to the mean $\mu$, the fluctuation term $\frac{\sigma^2}{2}$ has an outsized impact on the growth rate, as the impact of negative fluctuations on the log outweighs that of equally-sized positive fluctuations. We can easily demonstrate this since even when gaining and losing at the same rate the overall outcome will be net negative: $(1 - \epsilon_t)(1 + \epsilon_t) = 1 - \epsilon_t^2 < 1$ for any $\epsilon_t > 0$. This dynamic is referred to as \textit{variance drain} \parencite{messmore1995variance} or \textit{volatility drag} \parencite{spitznagel2021safe}. Reducing the magnitude of fluctuations is the largest lever for increasing the growth rate. 
This connects directly to the pooling result. Following \textcite{peters2022ergodicity}, with $K$ firms whose growth increments $\delta_i$ are independently drawn from the same distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and who share their absolute increments equally each period, the time-average growth rate of each firm becomes

\begin{gather}
    \label{eq:pooling_improvement}
    g = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2K}
\end{gather}

As the number of firms $K$ in a conglomerate increases, the drag imposed by fluctuations decreases. In the limit $K \to \infty$ each firm is able to realize the ensemble average $\mu$ at every period. Note that the closed-form result in equation~\ref{eq:pooling_improvement} requires identical $(\mu, \sigma)$ across pool members and equal sharing weights. 

The identity is exact for log-normal returns and holds to second order in general: compound growth is the mean log return less half its variance. Half the variance reduction that pooling in a conglomerate delivers is therefore its gain in compound growth, which \textcite{booth1992diversification} term the ``diversification return'', and which is widely used in practice \parencite{erb2006strategic}. It also follows that a conglomerative arrangement whose value lies in the fluctuations it removes is undervalued by any evaluation that averages over typical periods:
its benefit lies in those realizations that an ensemble average weights by their probability and a single firm's history weights by their consequence \parencite{peters2019ergodicity}.

The same arithmetic is known in population biology, where what selection maximizes in a fluctuating environment is the geometric mean of fitness rather than its arithmetic mean, and variance lowers it by the same second-order term \parencite{lewontin1969population, gillespie1974natural}. Pooling between firms is, in that vocabulary, a bet-hedging arrangement: it gives up nothing in mean and buys a reduction in variance.

Of course there are limits to how large a conglomerate can grow, given that management cost --- particularly for coordination --- increase with scope \parencite{coase19883}. Any increase in conglomerate size raises management cost while also raising the growth rate for all member firms included. Again, one can think of this cooperation as a hedge, or an insurance against unfavorable growth increments, the premium for which is the cost of coordination. There is a trade-off, and a conglomerate size, though certainly varying, which optimizes it. 

This concept is related to portfolio diversification but qualitatively distinct. In contrast to the latter, pooling also improves the growth rates of the entities that make up the portfolio, in addition to producing higher portfolio-level growth than diversification alone. A pure portfolio strategy solely based on diversification has an expected growth rate of as shown in Equation \ref{eq:diversification}, while conglomeration provides access to internal resource transfers, which allows for an expected growth rate as shown in \ref{eq:pooling_improvement}, which is larger than diversification for $N > 1$. We define this cooperative diversification as a cooperative arrangement that increases the time-average growth rate of each constituent unit under multiplicative dynamics, rather than merely reducing variance at the portfolio level. The  difference between pooling and standard diversification, which can be achieved by simply allowing shareholders to diversify their own portfolios across independent firms, is important. When shareholders hold diversified portfolios, they reduce their own exposure to firm-specific risk, but each firm's growth trajectory remains unchanged. If firm $i$ suffers a negative shock, $i$'s size contracts regardless of how $j$ performs. Shareholders experience the average outcome, but the firms themselves do not.

Conglomerate formation enables something qualitatively different: internal resource transfers can move capital from better-performing to worse-performing units, directly altering each unit's growth path. Note that this does not even require the worse-performing firm to suffer losses. As seen in Figure \ref{fig:log}, this mechanism also works when both constituent firms of a conglomerate experience $\delta_{it} > 1$. When $i$ underperforms while $j$ prospers, the conglomerate can channel resources to $i$, partially offsetting its decline. This is not merely risk-sharing from the perspective of a portfolio holder, but an alteration of the underlying growth dynamics of the constituent firms. 

\section{Model}
\label{sec:Model}
\subsection{Growth Process}
We employ an agent-based model to study conglomerate formation and its competitive effects.\footnote{All code, calibrated parameter values, and instructions for reproducing the simulations and figures are available in the replication package at \href{https://github.com/SimonBloethner/conglomerates}{https://github.com/SimonBloethner/conglomerates}.} This methodological choice reflects three features of the phenomenon under study. First, firm heterogeneity is essential: firms differ in their market positions, growth histories, and conglomerate memberships, and these differences drive merger decisions. A representative-agent approach would obscure the distributional dynamics central to our research questions. Second, the system exhibits strategic interdependence: firm $i$'s decision to merge with firm $j$ depends on $j$'s current conglomerate composition, which itself depends on prior merger decisions throughout the economy. This generates path-dependent dynamics where history matters for market structure. Hence, each firm's merger decision depends on the current state of all other firms and conglomerates, which depends on the complete history of shocks and decisions. The number of feasible merger configurations grows combinatorially with the number of firms and markets, precluding analytical tractability. In such settings, simulation is not merely a convenience but a necessity for studying how micro-level pooling incentives aggregate into macro-level market structure \parencite{axtell2025agent}. 

Firm $i$ belongs to one market $m(i)$ and has size $s_{it}$, its accumulated capital at time $t$. All firms start at size one and unaffiliated. The $M$ markets are the economy's industries, and a conglomerate is a set of firms in distinct markets, so that by construction it is neither a horizontal nor a vertical combination.

Each period every firm draws a log growth shock

\begin{gather}
    x_{it} = g + \sigma_m \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \varepsilon_{it} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F,
\end{gather}

where $F$ belong to one of three families with the same interquartile range: normal, Laplace, and Student-t with three degrees of freedom. While the Laplace family is the empirical regularity for firm growth rates \parencite{stanley1996scaling,bottazzi2006explaining}, the normal and t$_3$ families bracket it with thinner and heavier tails. Each family enters as a location-scale family, $\varepsilon$ its standard member and $\sigma_m$ the scale, set so that the interquartile range of $x_{it}$ equals the market's volatility parameter $q_m$. Holding the interquartile range fixed means that the three families agree on the body of the distribution and differ only in the tails. Market volatilities $q_m$ are drawn once per economy and rescaled linearly onto $[0.10, 0.30]$, so markets differ in volatility and in nothing else.\footnote{
The interquartile range of $\varepsilon$ is $2F^{-1}(0.75)$ by symmetry, so $\sigma_m = q_m/(2F^{-1}(0.75))$: $q_m/1.349$ for the normal, $q_m/1.386$ for the Laplace and $q_m/1.530$ for t$_3$. $\sigma_m$ is a standard deviation only in the normal case. At $q_m = 0.2$ the standard deviations of $x_{it}$ are $0.148$, $0.204$ and $0.226$.} The range is a slice of that span, not the whole of it, which runs over seven orders of magnitude. Shocks are not truncated and in the baseline are independent across firms and markets. We add a robustness check with cross-market correlation of $0.3$, imposed through a Gaussian copula so that the marginals are unchanged.

By construction $\mathbb{E}[x_{it}] = g$, such that every firm in every market has the same time-average growth rate, and we set $g = 0.055$. The ensemble-average rate of market $m$ is $\mu_m = \log \mathbb{E}[e^{x}] = g + \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma_m \varepsilon}]$, which equals $g + \sigma_m^2/2$ for normal shocks and $g - \log(1 - \sigma_m^2)$ for Laplace shocks. Note that for t$_3$ shocks the ensemble average does not exist, while the time average $g$ is unaffected. The gap $\mu_m - g$ is the volatility premium of market $m$, the growth that an ensemble of trajectories realizes and a single trajectory does not.

We measure each firm against its own market. Let $w_{it}$ be firm $i$'s share of market capital at the start of period $t$ and


$$G_{mt} = \sum_{i \in m} w_{it}\, e^{x_{it}}$$

the market's realized capital-weighted growth factor. A firm's relative log return is

$$\tilde r_{it} = x_{it} - \log G_{m(i)t},$$

and an unaffiliated firm grows as $s_{it+1} = s_{it} e^{\tilde{r}}$. Since $\sum_i w_{it} e^{\tilde{r}_{it}} = 1$ by construction, the total capital of a market of unaffiliated firms is constant and sizes are market shares up to the factor $N$. We normalize every market to mean size one. The common drift $g$ cancels exactly in the relative return, which is why its level cannot affect shares or any decision based on them: the three families and the volatility range are the only features of the shock process that matter.

Two properties of relative returns drive what follows. First, by Jensen's inequality applied to $\sum_i w_{it} e^{\tilde{r}_{it}} = 1$, the capital-weighted mean of $\tilde{r}$ is non-positive in every market and period, and to second order

$$\mathbb{E}[\tilde r_{it}] \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_m^2 \bigl(1 - \mathrm{HHI}_{mt}\bigr),$$

with $HHi_{mt} = \sum_i w_{it}^2$. A single trajectory loses share to its market at a rate that is the market's volatility premium scaled by how far the market is from a monopoly. This can be interpreted as the share version of the volatility drag of Section~\ref{sec:Background}. Second, relative returns within a market are correlated through $G_{mt}$: a large draw for one firm lowers the relative return of every other firm in the market. This is a cross-sectional factor, not a common shock, and it survives into the results as the clustering of floor hits within market-periods.

A firm whose size falls below a fraction $c$ of its market's mean size is lifted to that level, the mean being taken after the lift, since minimum viable scale is relative to the market as it stands once entry has happened.\footnote{footnote: lifting a firm raises the mean and hence the floor, so the level is the fixed point of $c \times$ mean, found by iterating within the period.} We count each such event as a floor hit. A firm at the floor is a going concern recapitalized at minimum viable scale, and the floor-hit count is the model's entry rate, while its recorded history of returns stays with it. The floor is the reflecting barrier of \textcite{levy1996power}: a multiplicative process reflected at a fraction $c$ of the mean has a stationary Pareto tail with exponent $1/(1 - c)$ \parencite{gabaix1999zipf}, and we calibrate $c$ so that the unaffiliated economy reproduces the empirical firm-size tail. Limited liability holds for everyone: no firm can lose more than its capital. 

This yields capital share distributions rather than conventional revenue-based market shares. This approach is well-grounded in industrial organization theory. The \textcite{kreps1983quantity} framework demonstrates that when firms first choose production capacity and then compete on price, the equilibrium outcome equals Cournot competition—capacity determined by capital investment is the strategically relevant variable, with sales as the subsequent outcome. \textcite{dixit1980role} reinforces this logic through entry deterrence theory: a firm's capital stock represents a credible commitment that shapes post-entry competition, making accumulated investment the determinant of sustainable competitive position. \textcite{sutton1991sunk} extends this insight, showing that in industries with significant sunk costs, there exists a lower bound on concentration determined by the scale of capital investments required for viable operation. In addition, we posit that capital is what makes future production, and hence the ability to serve customers, possible, so a firm's share of a market's capital measures its claim on that market's future rather than its sales in the period just past.

Empirically, asset-based concentration measures are standard practice in several domains. \textcite{scitovsky1955economic} explicitly discusses choosing between ``employment, sales, value added, value of total assets, and 'net capital assets''' as concentration measures, noting that asset-based measures may capture different aspects of market power than sales-based measures. \textcite{dang2018measuring} survey 100 empirical articles and find that total assets ranks alongside sales as among the most common firm size proxies, with the appropriate choice depending on context. \textcite{kwon2024100} use both asset share and sales share as parallel concentration measures in their century-long study of U.S. corporate concentration, finding correlation exceeding $0.99$ between alternative measures. In banking, the Federal Reserve uses deposits—a liability-side capital measure—rather than revenue for merger concentration analysis, as deposits proxy for lending capacity. The European Central Bank similarly reports banking concentration using total assets. In electricity markets, FERC calculates merger HHIs based on generation capacity shares rather than revenue \parencite{roach2002measuring}. In international trade, UNCTAD ranks multinational enterprises by foreign assets, and gravity models routinely use FDI stocks as measures of market presence \parencite{ramondo2013trade}. The theoretical rationale is consistent across these applications: accumulated capital represents credible commitment and determines cost structure, while sales fluctuate with transitory demand conditions. For analyzing market concentration dynamics, capital-based measures capture sustainable competitive position rather than period-specific outcomes.

\subsection{Conglomerates\label{sec:conglomerates}}

A conglomerate $C$ is a set of $K$ firms, at most one per market, that pool a fraction $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ of their gross returns. Let $S_{ct}$ be the unit's capital and $\omega_{it = s_{it} / S_{ct}}$ member $i$'s weight within it. Each member contributes $\alpha$ of its gross relative return in capital units to a pool. Afterwards the pool is charged a coordination cost $\Phi(K) S_{ct}$ and the remainder is distributed in proportion to size. A member's realized gross growth factor is therefore

$$\delta_{it} = (1-\alpha)\, e^{\tilde r_{it}} + \alpha \sum_{j \in C} \omega_{jt}\, e^{\tilde r_{jt}} - \Phi(K), \qquad s_{i,t+1} = s_{it}\,\delta_{it}.$$

Pooling operates on gross factors: a member keeps $(1 - \alpha)$ of its own factor and receives $\alpha$ of the unit's capital-weighted factor. With $\alpha = 0$ the expression reduces to standalone growth minus the coordination cost, so at $\alpha = 0$ no conglomerate can pass the consent test of Section~\ref{sec:decision}, and the baseline is an economy of unaffiliated firms facing the same opportunity process as every other scenario. With $\alpha = 1$ all members grow at the unit's rate. Within a period pooling is a pure redistribution among members. However, across periods it changes each member's growth path, which is the sense in which it differs from diversification by the firms' owners.

Section~\ref{sec:Background} gives a firm's time-average growth to second order as its mean log return less half its variance. Pooling leaves the mean where it was, since every member has the same drift $g$, and changes the variance, so the gain in time-average growth of member $i$ from a unit $C$ operating at rate $\alpha$ is, to the same order,

$$\Delta_i(C,\alpha) \;=\; \tfrac12\Big[(1-\alpha)\,V(\epsilon_i) \;+\; \alpha\sum_{j\in C}\omega_j\,V(\epsilon_j) \;-\; V(y_i)\Big] \;-\; \Phi(K), \qquad y_i \;=\; (1-\alpha)\,\epsilon_i \;+\; \alpha\sum_{j\in C}\omega_j\,\epsilon_j ,$$

where $\varepsilon_i$ is the fluctuation of $i$'s relative return around its mean, $V(\cdot)$ a variance and $y_i$ the fluctuation $i$ carries as a member. The three terms in the bracket are the variance the firm gives up, the variance of the partners it takes on, and the variance of the pooled return it ends up with. For members of equal volatility the first two collapse to $V(\varepsilon_i)$ and the gain is half the variance reduction. The second term is where the terms of the contract bite when volatilities differ: rearranging, $\Delta_i$ contains the transfer $1/2 \alpha [\sum_i \omega_i V(\varepsilon_i) - V(\varepsilon_j)]$, which is negative for a member whose variance is above the unit's capital-weighted average and positive for one below it. Pooling gross factors moves arithmetic growth from volatile members to calm ones, so a volatile member pays for its calmer partners' protection as well as its own and since $\alpha$ is one rate for the whole unit and there are no side payments, it is the volatile side that can refuse. For $K$ members of equal volatility $\sigma^2$ with independent shocks and equal weights the expression reduces to

$$\Delta(K,\alpha) \;=\; \tfrac12\,\alpha(2-\alpha)\,\sigma^2\Big(1-\frac1K\Big) \;-\; \Phi(K),$$

which at $\alpha = 1$ is the $\sigma^2/2 · (1 - 1/K)$ of Section~\ref{sec:Background}. The gross gain rises in $\alpha$ throughout $[0, 1]$, so a symmetric unit pools fully; an interior sharing rate can arise only where unequal members must agree, which is what the unanimity rule of Section~\ref{sec:decision} turn on. The marginal gain of the $K$-th member is

$$\Delta(K,\alpha)-\Delta(K-1,\alpha) \;=\; \tfrac12\,\alpha(2-\alpha)\,\sigma^2\Big[\frac{1}{K-1}-\frac{1}{K}\Big] \;-\; \big[\Phi(K)-\Phi(K-1)\big],$$

which falls as $1/K^2$ while the marginal cost rises. With unequal weights the entrant's size matters as much as its count. An entrant of weight $\omega$ into a unit of $K - 1$ equal incumbents lowers each incumbent's pooled variance by an amount that vanishes with $\omega$, $\alpha \sigma^2 \omega / (K - 1)$ to first order, so a recruit that arrives at the floor brings the incumbents a small fraction of the equal-weight figure.

The cost $\Phi(K)$ is a uniform growth drag: every member's capital is charged the same rate, so the cost falls on members in proportion to size. We use four functional forms,

$$\Phi(K) \in \bigl\{\, b_0 K^{b_1},\quad b_0 + b_1 K,\quad b_0 + b_1 K + b_2 K^2,\quad b_0 e^{b_1 K} \,\bigr\},$$

power law, linear, quadratic and exponential, with parameters set so that all four charge $\Phi(40) = 0.0017$ per period. The literature on management diseconomies gives a theoretical case for costs that rise with scope \parencite{coase19883,williamson1967hierarchical} but little guidance on their shape \parencite{canback2006diseconomies}. The four chosen forms span initially concave, linear, convex and strongly convex schedules at a common level. The power-law form is the main case. The other three are reported in the appendix, where the finding is that the shape does not matter for scope once the level is held fixed. We abstract from fixed costs and returns to scale, which create merger incentives that are already well understood, so that pooling is the only reason to merge.

Under an alternative equal sharing rule, run as a robustness block, the net pool is split into $K$ equal amounts rather than in proportion to size; a small member can then be assigned a loss larger than its capital, which limited liability caps at its capital, the shortfall falling on the other members in proportion to theirs.

\subsection{Merging and Splitting\label{sec:decision}}

Firms form, join and leave conglomerates on a single criterion, applied to every proposal and to every membership: a firm agrees to an arrangement only if its own time-average growth, estimated from the recent past, would be higher under that arrangement than under its current one. Firms are forward-looking in the only way the model allows: they extrapolate, in the manner of \textcite{simon1955behavioral}, that the future will behave like the past they have observed, and estimate the value of pooling from realized history, as merger due diligence does. In this economy the extrapolation is roughly right, since shocks are stationary.

Each period every firm independently initiates a proposal with probability $p$. An initiator draws a target market uniformly among the markets its unit does not yet occupy (all markets but its own, if unaffiliated), and a target firm uniformly within that market. If the target belongs to a conglomerate, the two units merge as wholes. A proposal is feasible only if the merged set still has at most one firm per market. Feasibility is a constraint, not a decision. The search is uniform and $p$ is not calibrated to anything, which is why we vary it from 0.05 to 1.

Let $\hat{C}$ be the prospective merged set with $\hat{K}$ members and cost $\Phi(\hat{K})$, and let $l$ be the length of the window of past periods that firms consult. For each firm $i$ in $\hat{C}$ and each period $\tau$ in the window ${t - l, \ldots, t - 1}$, define the firm's demeaned relative return

$$\epsilon_{i\tau} = \tilde r_{i\tau} - \bar{\tilde r}_i + g, \qquad \bar{\tilde r}_i = \tfrac{1}{l}\textstyle\sum_{\tau} \tilde r_{i\tau},$$

and the fluctuation it would have carried in period $\tau$ as a member of $\tilde{C}$,

$$y_{i\tau} = (1-\alpha)\,\epsilon_{i\tau} + \alpha \sum_{j \in \hat C} \omega_{j\tau}\,\epsilon_{j\tau},$$

with the weights $\omega_i \tau$ taken from the members' sizes in period $\tau$. The firm's estimate of its gain is the $\Delta_{i}$ computed on the window, with sample variances $\hat{V}$ in place of $V$ and the prospective size $\hat{K}$ in place of $K$:

$$\tilde\Delta_i(\hat C, \alpha) = \tfrac12 \Bigl[ (1-\alpha)\,\hat V(\epsilon_i) + \alpha \sum_{j \in \hat C} \bar\omega_j\,\hat V(\epsilon_j) - \hat V(y_i) \Bigr] - \Phi(\hat K)$$

This is the replayed log growth of the window expanded to second order in the fluctuations; the exact replay is the bounded variant described below, and the two coincide as $l$ grows. \footnote{for symmetric shocks the first omitted term is of fourth order. On the traced runs the omitted terms are a median $1-2$\% of the gain, within $\pm 5$ \%of it in nine windows out of ten under normal shocks and $\pm 8$\% under Laplace, and within $-19$\% to $+35$\% under t$_3$, where rare windows with an extreme draw carry the error (appendix).} The rate $\alpha$ is the rate at which the merged unit would operate: the common rate in the main design, and in the endogenous block the rate of the larger of the two units, or the starting rate when two unaffiliated firms meet. A proposal is accepted if and only if

$$\tilde\Delta_i(\hat C, \alpha) > \max\bigl\{0,\ \tilde\Delta_i(C_i, \alpha)\bigr\} \quad \text{for every } i \in \hat C,$$

where $C_i$ is firm $i$'s current unit and $\tilde{\Delta}_i(C_i, \alpha) = 0$ for an unaffiliated firm. Every firm on both sides holds a veto, two-firm mergers are tested like any other, and a merger that raises the unit's total pool while lowering some member's growth fails.

In words, each firm compares the volatility it bears on its own with the volatility it would bear inside the unit, over the same $l$ periods and the same shocks, and consents if the reduction, net of the coordination cost, is worth more than its present arrangement, the better of its current unit and standing alone. The statistic is the firm's own: $\varepsilon_{i\tau}$ is built from the firm's own shock $\tilde{r}_{i\tau}$, which every firm observes whatever its affiliation, because pooling is an accounting layer on top of the shocks, and the same construction with the firm's current unit in place of $\hat{C}$ gives $\tilde{\Delta}_i(C_i, \alpha)$. The three terms and the transfer between members of unequal volatility are those of Section~\ref{sec:conglomerates}; on a window of $l$ periods they are estimated, not known, and for $K$ equal members and a long window the estimate converges to $1/2 \alpha (2 - \alpha) \sigma^2 (1 - 1 / K) - \Phi(K)$. Three things are assumed of the firm. It observes $l$ periods of its own and its prospective partners' relative returns and the cost schedule; it knows that compound growth is mean less half variance, which is the arithmetic of log returns rather than a theory; and it takes the window as representative of what is to come. This is what an analyst valuing a diversification arrangement does: volatilities and covariances are estimated from a lookback window of returns, the variance of the combined position is computed from them, and the gain in compound growth is read off as half the variance reduction, the ``diversification return'' of \textcite{booth1992diversification}, a practitioner's quantity \parencite{erb2006strategic} before it was a theorist's. What is specific to this model is only that the firm has no information beyond that window. It does not know the shock distribution, and holds no view of the future other than the past. The demeaning is deliberate: over $l$ periods the realized mean of a prospective partner is a noisy estimate of a drift that is the same for everyone, so comparing levels would have firms accept partners that happened to have a good window and reject partners that happened to have a bad one, and the common component of the window would swamp the diversification gain. Firms therefore judge pooling on fluctuations around each firm's own window mean, recentred at the common rate $g$. The rule does not presume that pooling pays: whether $\tilde{\Delta}$ is positive for every member of a prospective unit depends on their volatilities, their sizes, the cost and the window. As a bounded variant we also run a rule that estimates the same quantity by replaying the window, member by member, and taking the sample mean of the log difference between pooled and standalone growth; it uses the same information, the same window and the same veto, and converges to $\tilde{\Delta}$ as $l$ grows, but at $l = 50$ its sampling error is an order of magnitude larger than the gain a marginal member brings, and it is biased low by a fifth to a third of the gain: within a demeaned window a firm's weight is high after above-mean returns, which the demeaning then offsets with below-mean ones, so the replay's first-order term, zero in expectation, is negative in most finite windows (appendix). A third rule, the exact replay with that first-order term removed and therefore exact in expectation, reproduces the moment rule's outcomes to within a tenth of a member (appendix), so nothing below depends on the second-order approximation; we keep the moment rule as the base case because it is the estimate practitioners make, not a statistical device. 

A member whose tenure in its unit exceeds $l$ periods applies the same test every period and leaves if $\tilde{\Delta}_i(C_i, \alpha) < 0$. The lock-in of $l$ periods applies to every new member and, when two units merge, to every member of the merged unit. A unit that falls to one member dissolves. The second route out of a unit is the floor: a member lifted to the floor leaves its unit in that period. The two exits differ in kind. A voluntary exit is a judgement that pooling did not pay. A floor exit is the loss of a configuration, whose capital is gone whether pooling delayed the loss or not. 

In the main design $\alpha$ is a parameter common to all units. In the endogenous-$\alpha$ block each unit carries its own rate $\alpha_C$ and renegotiates it every period on the grid ${0, 0.05, \ldots, 1}$ by the same demeaned criterion as the membership test: it moves to a new rate only if every member would have gained from it over the window, and among such rates takes the one that raises the least-gaining member's growth the most, unanimity first and maximin second. New units start at $\alpha_C = 0.1,$ a merged unit takes the rate of the larger one, and every consent and exit test evaluates a unit at the rate it operates at. The order of events within a period is given in the appendix (Algorithm 1). 

\subsection{Calibration and Design\label{sec:calibration}}

Table~\ref{tab:parameters} lists every parameter of the main block. The economy has $M = N = 50$ and runs for $T = 11,000$ periods, of which the first $8,000$, the relaxation from the artificial initial state of equal unaffiliated firms, are discarded; a rerun of four headline cells to $16,000$ periods shows no drift in any statistic over the retained window (appendix).

The floor coefficient is calibrated to the empirical firm-size tail. \textcite{axtell2001zipf} reports a Pareto exponent of $1.06$ for US firms. At $\alpha = 0$ the economy is a floored multiplicative process whose tail exponent is $1/(1 - c)$ asymptotically, but at $N = 50$ the finite-sample Hill estimate \parencite{hill1975simple} on the top decile of shares lies below that value, so we calibrate $c$ by simulation: $c^* = 0.127$ is the value at which the median Hill exponent of the unaffiliated Laplace economy equals $1.06$. Because the floor is a fraction of the mean, and hence a share of $c/N$, the calibration depends on $N$. For the economy-size robustness block we recalibrate to $c^* = 0.069$ at $N = 25$ and $0.158$ at $N = 100$. The three families reproduce the target at $c^*$: Hill exponents of $1.07$ (normal), $1.06$ (Laplace) and $1.00$ (t$_3$): the calibration is to the light-tailed families, and the same floor gives a slightly heavier tail the heavier the shocks ($0.96$ at $\nu = 2.5$). A block with $c \in {0.10, 0.35}$ shows that $c$ alone sets the tail.

The time-average rate is set to $g = 0.055$. Its level is immaterial: as shown in Section~\ref{sec:conglomerates} it cancels from every share, and it enters the decision rule only as the recentring constant, where its level has a second-order effect. The window is $l = 50$ periods in the main block and varied from $20$ to $500$ in a block of its own. The search probability is $p = 0.05$ and is varied to $0.2$ and $1$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The unaffiliated economy}\label{sec:results-baseline}

At $\alpha = 0$ no proposal passes the consent test and the economy is one of 2,500 unaffiliated firms. Its size distribution has the tail that \textcite{axtell2001zipf} documents for US firms, by calibration: the Hill exponent of within-market shares is 1.07 for normal shocks, 1.06 for Laplace and 1.00 for $t_3$, against the target of 1.06 at which the floor coefficient was set. The floor is what sets it. In the floor-level block the exponent is 1.01 at $c = 0.10$ and 1.52 at $c = 0.35$ in every family, and the Levy–Solomon value $1/(1-c) = 1.15$ is the asymptote that a market of fifty firms does not reach. Within-market Herfindahl indices are 0.17–0.20 and the largest ten per cent of firms hold 65–68\% of capital. The search process runs at the same rate as in every other scenario, 125 proposals per period, and none is accepted; the comparison between zero and positive sharing therefore holds the opportunity process fixed and varies only the contract.

Entry is frequent. A standalone firm hits the floor in about 9\% (normal), 12\% (Laplace) and 13\% ($t_3$) of firm-periods: with these volatilities the typical firm is recapitalized at minimum scale roughly once every eight to eleven periods, and more often the heavier the tails. This rate is the hazard that pooling will be measured against.

\subsection{Why Conglomerates Stay Small}\label{sec:results-scope}

At any positive sharing rate conglomerates form at once and come to hold most of the economy's capital, but they stay small (Figure~\ref{fig:scope}, Table~\ref{tab:scope}). Scope rises monotonically in $\alpha$ and reaches at $\alpha = 0.3$ under half of the equal-members reference $K^*$ under normal shocks, a third under Laplace and a tenth under $t_3$; the $t_3$ reference sits at the cap of $M = 50$ markets at $\alpha \geq 0.3$, so its ratio is an upper bound on how close formation gets. The ordering across families is the result to explain: the reference scope rises steeply with the weight of the tails, because that is where the volatility premium to be recovered is largest, while the scope that forms falls with it, because heavier tails raise the rate at which members are lost to the floor and lower what a window of returns can verify. Pairs are accepted whenever proposed, and acceptance is higher the heavier the tails; what firms refuse is the next member once a unit exists, and that refusal, not the environment, is where most of the distance lies.

Costs are not what stops unit growth: at the scope that forms the coordination cost is one to three per cent of the pooling gain, and by the marginal calculus of Section~\ref{sec:Background} the $K$-th member pays for itself up to $K \approx 12$ (normal), 16 (Laplace) and 17 ($t_3$). Doubling the cost level costs about 0.6 members at $\alpha = 0.3$, and at twenty times the calibrated level, where $K^*$ has fallen to 4–5, scope has fallen to 2.8: units do not reach the planner's optimum even when it is brought down to where they already were (appendix).

What stops them is consent on members whose gain to the incumbents is too small to be seen (Figure~\ref{fig:consent}). Acceptance falls monotonically with the size of the prospective unit, from 100\% for a pair to 59–69\% for a fourth member, 48–54\% for a fifth, 20–31\% for an eighth and 4–13\% for a twelfth at $\alpha = 0.3$, at the baseline search rate and at $p = 1$ alike. The member who binds is an incumbent, and the recruit on offer is small: half of the proposals at $K \geq 4$ bring a standalone just recapitalized at the floor, whose weight in the prospective unit is 0.06–0.07 at $K = 5$ against the 0.2 of an equal member and 0.02–0.03 at $K = 8$. An incumbent's diversification gain from such a recruit is a third of the equal-weight gain of Section~\ref{sec:Background} at $K = 5$ and a quarter at $K = 8$, and net of the marginal cost it reaches zero at $K \approx 7$ under normal shocks and 8–9 under Laplace and $t_3$. The binding member's estimated gain, the smallest over members of $\tilde\Delta_i(\hat C) - \max\{0, \tilde\Delta_i(C_i)\}$, tracks that margin shifted down by $2$--$5\cdot10^{-5}$ --- unanimity takes the minimum of $K$ noisy estimates --- and so crosses zero two members earlier, between the fifth and the seventh. Firms are not reluctant to pool: they accept every pair, and more of everything under heavier tails. They decline recruits that bring the unit nothing, and the recruits that a floored economy supplies bring little. The one other kind of proposal, the merger of two existing units (nine in ten of the proposals whose entrant side holds a fifth or more of the capital at $K \geq 8$), fails for the other reason: when the two units differ in volatility, pooling moves arithmetic growth from the volatile unit to the calm one, and it is the volatile unit's members whose consent fails, in 67–87\% of such proposals when it is the incumbent; acceptance of unit mergers at $K \geq 8$ is 1–23\% against 13–43\% for a standalone entrant of the same weight. The loggain rule, which estimates the same gain by a replayed sample mean, sees less again --- it accepts 38–42\% of fourth members and 13–17\% of eighth, and $K$ is 0.6–1.8 members smaller (Table~\ref{tab:scope}) --- because on a demeaned window of fifty periods its estimate is both noisier and biased low by a fifth to a third of the gain; an exact replay with that bias removed behaves like the moment rule to within a tenth of a member.

The window is the direct test of this bound. Memory lengthens scope slowly and without saturation (Table~\ref{tab:window}): a longer window shrinks the sampling error of the second moments and lets more marginal members pass. Each step of the grid $l \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$, a factor of two to two and a half, adds 0.3–0.5 members at $\alpha = 0.3$ under Laplace shocks; a twenty-five-fold longer window raises scope from 4.8 to 6.6, still well under half of $K^*$ = 16, and under $t_3$ from 4.3 to 5.4. Under the moment rule voluntary exits are nil at every window length, while under the loggain rule they fall from about five per period at $l = 20$ to none beyond $l = 200$: the exits that a longer window removed were decisions taken on noise, and the exits that remain, 35–50 per period at every $l$, are deaths at the floor. The attrition that bounds scope under heavy tails is set by the floor, not by the firms' patience.

Whether that refusal is a shortfall depends on which planner is asked, and the answer turns on the capital weights of the members. The equal-members reference $K^*$ assumes every recruit is as large as the incumbents; the recruits that arrive are floor-sized. To the incumbents such a recruit is worth little --- at $K = 5$ about a third of the equal-weight gain (Table~\ref{app:A6}) --- while to the recruit admission is worth the whole variance cut and the hazard reduction of Proposition 3. A planner who weighs capital refuses with the incumbents; one who weighs firms admits everyone. The disagreement between the two is the disagreement between a unit of capital and a firm, and the contract offers no instrument --- one rate for the whole unit, no side payments --- by which the recruit could pay the incumbents for admission. The firms, who know their weights where no formula does, refuse; the distance to $K^*$ is for the most part the distance between a configuration of equal members and the configuration the economy offers. It is not a friction that more search would remove: the recruits on offer are small, the record on which they are judged is short, and the contract gives a recruit no way to pay for admission. It is a disequilibrium result in the strict sense --- the structure that forms is not the structure that would be chosen with full information, and it stays that way.

Turnover is high: members hit the floor in 1.4\%, 2.2\% and 2.8\% of member-periods at $\alpha = 0.3$ (normal, Laplace, $t_3$) and are ejected; mergers per period match floor exits per period in every cell of Table~\ref{tab:scope}, and voluntary exits are nil. Firms with heavier tails are not less willing to pool --- they accept more of the proposals that reach them (44\% against 29\% at $\alpha = 0.3$) and merge more often --- they are less able to stay pooled, because their members are recapitalized more often. The population of conglomerates is a steady state of churn. But churn is not what sets its level. Three further economies that share the seeds of the main block say what does (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). In the first, consent is switched off: every feasible proposal goes through and no one leaves by choice, so the units are as large as the floor and the one-firm-per-market constraint allow --- a control with no knowledge, not a behavioural alternative. Under normal and Laplace shocks these units hold 14–28 members, more than the equal-members reference itself ($K^*$ = 8–20): the environment, with the same attrition, permits units larger than the equal-members arithmetic recommends, and the whole distance from $K^*$ to the scope that forms is consent. Under $t_3$ the control stops at 12–22 members against $K^*$ = 20–50, so there attrition accounts for 44–71\% of the distance and consent for the rest. In the second economy every market has the same volatility, so that pooling moves no premium between members and the consent test is estimation alone; the conflict between members of unequal volatility over the premium that pooling redistributes takes at most 4\% of the distance. In the third, the loggain economy, the same gain is estimated by a sample mean; the estimate's finite-window bias and noise, verifiability in the strict sense, take 0.6–1.8 members. What the consent that remains refuses is not partners but recruits: with consent off, the extra mergers are almost all solo entries of floor-sized firms into existing units (86–89\% of the control's entries are at the floor), the recruits whose gain to the incumbents has fallen below the marginal cost (Figure~\ref{fig:consent}). The extra scope buys the economy little: in the control the pooled share is 0.02–0.11 higher, the tail exponent moves by at most 0.04 and the within-market Herfindahl by under 0.012, while members' floor-hit rates rise slightly, since the recruits are the firms most likely to be lifted. Scope is bounded by consent first and by what a window can verify second; attrition binds only under heavy tails; the transfer conflict is immaterial at these volatility ranges; and cost is the margin that would bind only in a calmer economy. The search block tests whether the rate at which recruits are found matters at all.

\subsection{Owners' growth and survival}\label{sec:results-owners}

The first two propositions are about structure. This one is about what membership is worth to the firm's owners, measured directly. For the 27 traced runs we observe every firm in every post-burn-in period, 7.5 million firm-periods per run, and compare the next-period owner return --- the log change in the firm's share net of any floor recapitalization, which is capital the owners did not earn --- of members and standalones in the same market and the same size decile, with affiliation taken at the start of the period. The comparison is within market and within size, so it does not reward members for being in calmer markets or for being large. Nor does it reward them for having passed the consent test: shocks are independent across periods, so a firm's next-period return does not depend on the window that admitted it, only on its market, its size through the floor, and whether it is pooled. The gain is a gain in the firm's share of its market, and since market capital is fixed it is, within each market, the co-market standalones' loss; what pooling adds to the economy is in Section~\ref{sec:results-distribution}.

The member–standalone gap in mean owner return is positive in every cell and every replication: 0.07, 0.16 and 0.51 percentage points per period at $\alpha = 0.05$, 0.1 and 0.3 under normal shocks, 0.10, 0.26 and 0.85 under Laplace, 0.27, 0.48 and 1.19 under $t_3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:owner_return}, Table~\ref{tab:owner}). The gap is close to linear in $\alpha$, rises with scope (units of two members gain least, units of five or more most: 0.28, 0.42, 0.54 pp for normal at $\alpha = 0.3$), is flat across size deciles except for a dip in the largest, and is ordered $t_3$ > Laplace > normal, 2.3 to 1 between the extremes at $\alpha = 0.3$, as the volatility premium $\tfrac12\alpha(2-\alpha)\sigma^2$ of Section~\ref{sec:Background} predicts. Part of the $t_3$ gain is the diversification of the within-market factor G that no firm can escape by staying put. (Loggain: 0.43, 0.82 and 1.14 pp at $\alpha = 0.3$; the moment rule's larger units gain a little more.)

The gain is partly ordinary growth and partly avoided ruin, in proportions set by the tails. Splitting the firm-periods by whether the firm reaches the floor in the next period (an exact accounting of the gap, Table~\ref{tab:owner}), 50\% of the gain at $\alpha = 0.3$ accrues in ordinary periods under normal shocks, 38\% under Laplace and 22\% under $t_3$: in those periods a member's owners grow 0.26, 0.32 and 0.26 pp faster than a standalone's in the same market and size decile. The remainder is that members reach the floor less often and lose less when they do. Standalone firms hit the floor in 19–23\% of standalone-periods, members in 1–3\% of member-periods: pooling cuts the hazard by a factor of seven to eighteen, more at higher $\alpha$ (7–11$\times$ at $\alpha = 0.05$, 12–18$\times$ at $\alpha = 0.3$). At $\alpha \leq 0.1$ the ordinary-period component is nil or slightly negative and the whole gain is ruin protection; the split is conservative for the ordinary component, since excluding floor periods removes more of the standalones' lower tail than of the members'. That is where the median loses sight of the mean: the median gap at $\alpha = 0.3$ is three fifths of the mean for normal and a third for $t_3$, and at $\alpha = 0.05$ it is within 0.02 pp of zero while the mean is clearly positive, because the benefit of pooling lives in the catastrophic periods that a typical window does not contain --- an institution whose value is avoided catastrophe is undervalued by any evaluation of typical periods, and most by the firms that need it most.

\subsection{The sharing rate firms choose}\label{sec:results-alpha}

The sharing rate has so far been imposed. When units set it themselves --- every period, by unanimity, on the demeaned window --- they choose to pool nearly everything they earn: starting from $\alpha = 0.1$, the adopted rate averages 0.91–0.93 with a median of 0.90 in every family, is never 0 and is 1 in 12–22\% of unit-periods (Table~\ref{tab:endogenous}). Read as an internal capital market, it is the share of operating cash flow that passes through headquarters and is reallocated across divisions rather than retained where it was earned (Stein, 1997; Lamont, 1997). With members of equal volatility the gain of Section~\ref{sec:Background} increases in $\alpha$ throughout, so a symmetric unit would pool fully; that units stop short of 1 is the mark of unanimity among unequal members, since a member whose volatility is below the unit's has less to gain from a higher rate and can veto it. Neither the dispersion of members' volatilities nor their level drives the choice: across 7,630 units the Spearman correlation of the adopted rate with the dispersion of members' volatilities is $-0.14$ to 0.00 and with their mean volatility 0.04–0.10. Under the loggain rule the latter was 0.14–0.26 and the adopted rate 0.80–0.85, not because the optimum differed but because only in volatile markets was the gain from a higher rate large enough to show through the window's noise. The scope these units reach, 14–18 members, is what the rate implies: a fixed $\alpha = 0.9$ gives 14.0–17.2 with the same pooled share, tail exponent and floor exits to within a few per cent, so renegotiation itself adds little, and the finding of the block is the rate. Going from $\alpha = 0.5$ to 0.9 doubles scope and cuts floor exits by a factor of three to six.

One block varies the terms on which firms pool rather than the environment they pool in, and the sharing rule decides whether conglomerates exist at all. Under equal dollar splits of the net pool (normal shocks, all four cost forms) mean scope is 2.1–3.2 at every sharing rate and pooled capital never exceeds 34\%, against 3.2–7.7 and up to 96\% under proportional sharing. Under an equal split the larger partner subsidizes the smaller in every period in which it does better, which it expects to do half the time, and refuses; the units that do form shed two to five members per period by choice, where proportional units shed none. The occasional ruin of a small member under equal sharing, charged to its partners, is not what holds scope at two or three; refusal is. The other contract term, the length of memory, is in Section~\ref{sec:results-scope} where it belongs to the verifiability bound.

\subsection{What pooling does to the size distribution}\label{sec:results-distribution}

The tail exponent rises with the sharing rate, and with nothing else. The Hill exponent of within-market shares is 1.06–1.08 (Laplace, normal) and 1.00 ($t_3$) at $\alpha = 0$ and within 0.01 of those values at $\alpha \leq 0.02$; it is 1.21, 1.20 and 1.12 at $\alpha = 0.3$ and 1.34, 1.34 and 1.22 at $\alpha = 0.5$, about 0.45 per unit of $\alpha$ up to there and faster beyond: 1.98, 1.94 and 1.78 at $\alpha = 0.9$, and 1.8–2.1 when units set their own rate (Figure~\ref{fig:hill}). Scope and the number of firms pooling do not enter: the search block triples mergers and raises scope by 35–65\% at the same rate without moving the exponent. Pooling thins the upper tail by the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:model-economy}. Every firm loses share to its market on average, by Jensen's inequality; a member loses it at (1 $- \alpha$) times the standalone rate, which is the gain of Proposition 3, so a member's expected growth is higher, not lower. What thins the tail is the compression of realized factors within a period: a member's lucky draw is shared with its partners and their unlucky draws with it, so the runs of large draws that carry a firm into the upper tail are rarer among members. Less dispersion of growth across firms in each period, and a higher time average for each firm across periods, are the same fact seen from two sides; it is the volatility tax that falls, not the firm's growth. The compression is visible in the members' own growth rates (appendix): at $\alpha = 0.3$ their dispersion is 0.77 of a standalone's, exactly what pooling gross returns at the units' weights predicts, and the cut falls on the lower tail --- the 1st percentile is 0.71 of a standalone's, the 99th 0.82 --- because a large own loss is floored by the partners' share while a large own gain passes through; the shape of the distribution is otherwise that of the shock family. Within markets firms thereby become more equal: the within-market Herfindahl index falls with $\alpha$ in every family. The share of the economy's capital held by the largest tenth of owning units, a conglomerate counted as one owner, is unchanged under normal shocks and rises moderately under Laplace and $t_3$ (appendix). Neither measure speaks to competition in a product market: a unit holds one firm per market, so horizontal concentration is excluded by construction, and what pooling concentrates is the ownership of capital across markets, not supply within any one.

Compression of sizes is not mobility of ranks. Pooling lowers rank mobility within markets rather than raising it --- the lag-100 rank correlation of a market's firms rises from 0.16–0.33 at $\alpha = 0$ to 0.25–0.45 at $\alpha = 0.3$ and the persistence of its five largest firms from 0.31–0.48 to 0.36–0.53 --- partly because fewer firms are recapitalized at the floor and partly because members' ranks move 9–20\% less than standalones' among going concerns; and it makes the top decile of owning units markedly more persistent, keeping 67–69\% of its membership over 100 periods against 32–49\% without pooling (Appendix~\ref{app:A17}). Pooling thus makes the size distribution within markets more equal and its ranks stickier, and makes the largest owners both somewhat larger and markedly more persistent.

Exits from conglomerates happen in bursts, which the pooling mechanism damps considerably (Figure~\ref{fig:burstiness}). Floor hits within a market-period are overdispersed: the index of dispersion is 2.4–3.0 (normal), 2.8–3.5 (Laplace) and 3.8–4.6 ($t_3$) against 0.88–0.94 for independent firms, and the worst market-period in a run sees 43–49 of 50 firms at the floor at once. The source is the cross-sectional factor G of Section~\ref{sec:model-economy}: one firm's large draw lowers every other firm's relative return in its market. The index falls with $\alpha$ in every family (3.0 $\to$ 2.4, 3.5 $\to$ 2.8, 4.6 $\to$ 3.8 from $\alpha = 0.05$ to 0.3) because members absorb part of the burst with capital from other markets.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:Discussion}
A recurring theme in the search for determinants of conglomerate formation is diversification to mitigate risks. As specialization of the Smithian \parencite{smith1776inquiry} and Ricardian \parencite{ricardo1817principles} nature allows us to increase our efficiency, and as we have steadily increased the degree of roundaboutness of production through capital intensification \parencite{bawerk1921kapital}, the potential downsides from miscalculation or shocks loom ever larger. Mitigating these, however, is also becoming simpler \parencite{felton1971conglomerate}, as both internal and external means of diversification \parencite{goldberg1973effect} are available, with their relative attractiveness depending on context. As demonstrated above, \textit{cooperative diversification} from conglomerate formation offers a reliable mitigation pathway and is applicable to many risk vectors. \par
Among these are macroeconomic or political shocks that are specific to certain geographic regions or sectors of the economy. The literature on international trade has picked up this theme arguing that cross-country foreign direct investment reduces country-specific risks \parencite{ramondo2010role} and even increases between less correlated markets \parencite{ramondo2013proximity}. Similarly, \textcite{amit1988concept} argue that conglomeration can diversify across business cycles, while \textcite{audretsch1989determinants} proposes that it can even be used to match different product life cycles. \par
Our model assumes independence of growth increments across markets, which maximizes the pooling benefit. In practice, markets exhibit varying degrees of correlation, particularly during economic crises when correlations tend to spike \parencite{forbes2002no}. \textcite{peters2022ergodicity} show that positive correlation reduces but does not eliminate pooling benefits, unless they are perfectly correlated. Our results therefore represent an upper bound on the incentives for conglomerate formation. This has two implications: first, conglomerates will preferentially form across markets with lower correlation structures; second, the competitive effects we identify will be attenuated in highly correlated environments. Empirically observed conglomerate formation rates and concentration effects should therefore be expected to be weaker than our simulations imply, particularly in periods or sectors with elevated cross-market correlations. \par
Our results shed light on the longstanding diversification discount debate. Early findings suggest that diversified firms trade at a discount relative to focused firms \parencite{lang1994tobins, berger1995diversification}, which has been taken as evidence that conglomeration destroys value. However, this evidence has been substantially challenged on methodological grounds: \textcite{campa2002explaining} show that the discount largely reflects endogeneity, and \textcite{villalonga2004diversification} finds a diversification premium when measurement biases are corrected. Our simulation results offer a structural interpretation. The non-linear relationship between pooling rate $\alpha$ and conglomerate outcomes, which is persistent across all our findings, implies that the value of conglomeration is non-monotonic: moderate pooling enhances growth rates for all constituent firms, but excessive pooling combined with coordination costs $\Phi(O_c)$ erodes these gains. This means that the diversification discount and premium can coexist in the same economy. Conglomerates that pool at rates near the optimum achieve higher time-average growth rates. Most telling for our framework, \textcite{kuppuswamy2016does} document that the diversification discount reversed during the 2007--2009 financial crisis, when conglomerates maintained access to internal capital while focused firms faced external financing constraints. This is precisely the setting where pooling benefits under multiplicative dynamics should manifest most strongly---when external options are limited and the variance drain on growth rates becomes the binding constraint. \par
A related point concerns the role of agency costs. The internal capital markets literature has documented how divisional rent-seeking corrupts capital allocation \parencite{scharfstein2000dark}, how headquarters with limited power misallocate capital across divisions \parencite{rajan2000cost}, and how free cash flow enables empire-building by self-interested managers \parencite{jensen1986agency}. These are real frictions. However, having seen the model, the reader will note that the coordination costs $\Phi(O_c)$ in Equations \ref{eq:power_law}--\ref{eq:exponential} capture the real resource costs of managing a larger organization in the tradition of \textcite{coase19883} and \textcite{williamson1967hierarchical}, which include the costs of communication, monitoring, and bureaucratic overhead that increase with conglomerate scope. These are conceptually distinct from agency costs, which arise from misaligned incentives between managers and shareholders. Our model deliberately abstracts from the latter. By showing that an intrinsic growth synergy from pooling exists even in the complete absence of agency problems, we establish a benchmark: the mathematical benefits of variance reduction under compounding are present regardless of governance quality. Agency costs then determine how much of this potential is realized in practice. The crisis evidence of \textcite{kuppuswamy2016does} is consistent with this interpretation, suggesting that survival pressures during crises align managerial and shareholder incentives, allowing the underlying pooling benefits to become visible. \par
The sensitivity of our results to the cost function specification (Figures \ref{fig:metrics}--\ref{fig:market_shares}) is itself informative. Power law costs, which scale initially slowly, permit larger but fewer conglomerates that reach their mobility ceiling at lower pooling rates. Exponential costs, by contrast, impose steep penalties on size, producing more numerous but smaller conglomerates with a wider effective range of $\alpha$. This suggests an empirically testable prediction: industries where coordination costs scale slowly with scope (e.g., financial holding companies managing independent portfolio firms) should exhibit fewer, larger conglomerates, while industries with rapidly scaling coordination costs (e.g., manufacturing with tightly coupled supply chains) should exhibit more, smaller ones. The fact that the direction of the pooling effect on concentration and mobility is robust across all four cost specifications, while the magnitude varies, indicates that the mechanism we identify is general even though the optimal conglomerate size is context-dependent. \par
A striking feature of our results is the discontinuity at $\alpha = 0$. Even minimal pooling fundamentally alters market dynamics away from the extreme concentration produced by pure Gibrat dynamics (Figure \ref{fig:market_shares}). Under the baseline of no pooling, multiplicative growth produces winner-take-all outcomes where a single firm absorbs nearly the entire market. This is the known consequence of random multiplicative processes \parencite{gibrat1931, gabaix1999zipf}. The introduction of any positive pooling rate breaks this dynamic, raising capital shares for lower quantiles and compressing inequality. The practical implication is that even loose conglomerate structures with modest internal resource transfers may be sufficient to generate the pro-competitive effects we document. This is consistent with the observation that venture capital firms enforce low pooling rates for their portfolio companies and incubator batches \parencite{boyd2023ergodic}, achieving meaningful variance reduction without heavy coordination overhead. More broadly, the pooling mechanism applies to any multiplicative process, extending beyond financial capital to physical and human capital, reputation, and other resources of interest to economic inquiry \parencite{pietronero2001explaining, sibani2020human}. 

\textcite{lewellen1971pure} offers a purely financial rationale for conglomeration, where firms merge in order to mitigate financing frictions and to provide internal capital markets \parencite{boguth2022dissecting}. Our results allow a sharper distinction between this coinsurance channel and the multiplicative dynamics mechanism. The coinsurance rationale operates through a static distributional effect---combining imperfectly correlated cash flows enhances debt capacity. The mechanism we identify operates through temporal compounding as formalized in Equation \ref{eq:pooling_improvement}: variance reduction directly increases the geometric growth rate of equity over time, independent of leverage effects. The two channels are complementary, but the growth-rate channel we document persists even when firms face no financing frictions. In addition, pooling can be used to reduce the highly volatile returns inherent to R\&D efforts, or share the costs associated with them \parencite{felton1971conglomerate}. 

The mechanism we identify operates alongside several other forces that govern organizational form. Selection determines which firms become conglomerates in the first place; managerial agency frictions can corrupt the internal capital reallocation that makes pooling effective; operational integration generates synergies that our model does not capture; market correlations attenuate the pooling benefit; and broader institutional context shapes the costs of coordination across borders, regulatory regimes, and ownership structures. Each of these forces operates simultaneously with the pooling mechanism, and the observed organizational form of any particular conglomerate reflects their joint operation. Our contribution is to isolate and characterize one of these forces under explicit assumptions. The crisis evidence of \textcite{kuppuswamy2016does} is suggestive of contexts where the pooling channel becomes more visible; systematic identification across non-crisis settings would require considerably more demanding empirical work than the existing literature provides.

We emphasize that our model speaks to market structure outcomes rather than welfare. While reduced concentration is often associated with improved consumer welfare in standard models, this connection requires assumptions about pricing, demand, and allocative efficiency that our framework does not incorporate. The welfare implications of the dynamics we identify therefore remain an open question, requiring models that integrate our growth-rate-optimization mechanism with explicit market interactions and a stance on the appropriate welfare criterion.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:Conclusion}
This article has developed a novel theoretical foundation for conglomerate mergers grounded in multiplicative dynamics and time-average growth-rate optimization. Building on the cooperation framework of \textcite{peters2022ergodicity}, who show that resource pooling is positive-sum under multiplicative dynamics, we have demonstrated that this mechanism provides a distinct rationale for conglomerate merger formation. By embedding their pooling result within an agent-based model of endogenous merger decisions, we show that growth-rate optimization through variance reduction can explain conglomerate formation even absent economies of scale, market power, tax motivations, or management incentive considerations — and that acting on these incentives compresses capital share distributions and increases rank mobility. Unlike portfolio diversification, which reduces a shareholder's risk exposure while leaving each firm's growth trajectory unchanged, cooperative diversification through pooling directly improves the growth dynamics of constituent firms. This extends and reinterprets the risk-reduction arguments of \textcite{amihud1981risk}, \textcite{lewellen1971pure}, and \textcite{mueller1969theory} within the framework appropriate for multiplicative processes, and complements the internal capital markets literature \parencite{stein1997internal, gertner1994internal} by identifying a growth-rate channel that operates even absent information asymmetries or financing frictions. 

Our simulations characterize the implications of the pooling mechanism under the assumption that firms act on the merger incentives it generates: capital share distributions compress across all quantiles, rank mobility increases, and the persistence of market dominance that characterizes pure multiplicative growth is reduced. The relationship between pooling and market outcomes is non-monotonic, while coordination costs impose a natural limit on conglomerate scope, producing an optimal pooling rate that depends on the cost structure. This non-monotonicity offers a model-based interpretation of the heterogeneous empirical evidence on the diversification discount \parencite{lang1994tobins, berger1995diversification, campa2002explaining, villalonga2004diversification}: conglomerates near the optimum implied by our model achieve higher time-average growth rates, while those that overshoot or face steep coordination costs do not. The robustness of the concentration-reducing and mobility-increasing direction across all four cost specifications, combined with the sensitivity of magnitude to cost structure, suggests that the mechanism is general even though its quantitative implications are context-dependent. 

Several avenues for future work present themselves. First, incorporating a pricing mechanism would allow for statements on consumer welfare, which our current framework, focused on market structure, cannot provide. Second, we assume normally distributed growth increments, whereas firm growth is empirically heavy-tailed \parencite{lahr2023fat, stanley1996scaling}, with negative shocks occurring more frequently than normality predicts \parencite{cont2001empirical, viscusi2014shifting}. Since large negative realizations have an outsized impact on the growth rate under multiplicative dynamics, fat-tailed distributions would likely strengthen the case for pooling, making it a natural extension. Finally, our assumption of uncorrelated markets represents an upper bound on pooling benefits. Empirically testing whether conglomerate mergers are more common across industries with lower return correlations, and whether conglomerate size distributions match the predictions of specific cost function shapes, would provide informative tests of the framework's predictions. The pooling mechanism we characterize is one among several forces governing organizational form. Selection, agency frictions, operational synergies, market correlations, and institutional context all enter the determination of equilibrium outcomes. Isolating the empirical signature of the pooling channel specifically remains a demanding identification problem, which we leave to subsequent work.
